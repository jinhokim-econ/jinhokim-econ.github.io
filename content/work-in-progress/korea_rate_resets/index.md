abstract: >-
  In Korea, hybrid mortgages reset automatically after five years, on a date fixed at origination. With little room for cash-out borrowing, the payment change is a scheduled cash-flow shock, not a refinancing choice. I exploit this quasi-experiment to identify borrowers’ responses during the 2022–23 rate hikes, using a credit bureau panel of Korean borrowers. The MPC out of the reset-induced increase in interest payments is small, about 0.1, well below what was found in reset studies that exploit rate cuts. Rather than cutting consumption, borrowers respond mainly on the debt margin, by refinancing or paying down their mortgages. This small average response masks an uneven incidence: borrowers who were liquidity constrained before the reset are much less able to refinance or extend maturity, and they instead lose access to unsecured credit and cut card spending by about 3 percent relative to unconstrained borrowers. The burden of monetary tightening thus falls on borrowers who lack access to debt adjustment, suggesting that targeted access to refinancing and maturity extension can mitigate its distributional costs.
